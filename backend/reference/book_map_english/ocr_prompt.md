## The taxonomy

The TAXONOMY below is a CLOSED list of chapters and their topics. Its IDs are this list's own: never use a number from your memory of the books. Copy every ID exactly as written. Never invent, merge, shorten or renumber an ID. Nothing deeper than the listed levels exists.

Chapter-ID prefixes tell you the area:
- R = Reading Skills (unseen passages)
- G = Grammar
- W = Writing Skills
- FF = First Flight prose chapters
- FP = First Flight poems
- FW = Footprints without Feet chapters

The words in square brackets after each topic are its KEY TERMS: the events, characters, quotations, ideas and question phrasings that belong to it. For literature, the topics follow the parts into which the textbook itself divides each chapter, so the key terms list what happens in each part. Use the key terms to choose between neighbouring topics.

TAXONOMY

R1 | Reading: Discursive Passage [an unseen passage of opinion, argument, description or reflection, 400–450 words, no data table]
  R1.1 | Literal comprehension [the answer is STATED in the passage and only has to be found: list or mention two points; give one reason or example from the text; complete the sentence with a suitable reason or information from a paragraph; True or False; which statement is true according to the passage; support or justify a statement or the writer's view with evidence from the text; cite one example; how, why or what when the passage states the answer; who, what, when, where]
  R1.2 | Inference and interpretation [the answer is NOT stated and must be worked out: infer, imply, conclude; what does the use of a phrase or statement suggest, reveal or tell us about the subject; state one inference; which statement best explains or reflects a concept; cause and effect that the passage does not state; assertion–reason; the main idea, message or central theme; a suitable title]
  R1.3 | Writer's tone, attitude, purpose and craft [tone, attitude or mood of the writer and its rationale; the writer's purpose; the comparison, analogy, metaphor or figure of speech the writer uses and what it conveys; the effect of the writer's choice of words or structure; style]
  R1.4 | Vocabulary in context [meaning of a word or phrase as used in the passage; what a word or phrase refers to; find or identify the word in paragraph X that means or indicates …; substitute the underlined word with a word from the passage; synonym, antonym; option closest in meaning; word that does NOT fit]
  R1.5 | Evaluation and application [the answer needs the student's OWN judgement beyond the passage: give your opinion; agree or disagree with reasons; apply an idea to a real-life situation; suggest a measure of your own; what would you do]

R2 | Reading: Case-based Factual Passage [an unseen factual or informational passage, 200–350 words, usually with a table, chart, graph, survey or other visual or numerical input]
  R2.1 | Literal comprehension [the answer is STATED in words in the text: complete the sentence with a reason from the text; True or False; list or mention points; give one reason; support or justify a statement with evidence from the text; how, why or what when the text states it]
  R2.2 | Data and visual interpretation [the answer is a NUMBER-based fact, wherever it appears (a table, chart, graph, infographic, survey, or figures written in the prose): read a value, percentage, rank, highest or lowest, the largest or smallest producer, a trend or a comparison of figures; calculate or compare figures; which option correctly reads the data]
  R2.3 | Inference and interpretation [the answer is NOT stated and must be worked out: infer, imply, conclude; state one inference from a statement or figure; what does the use of a phrase or a statistic suggest or emphasise; significance of a comparison; assertion–reason; the writer's view, tone or purpose in a factual text]
  R2.4 | Vocabulary in context [meaning of a word or phrase as used in the text; what a phrase refers to; identify the word or phrase in paragraph X that indicates or means …; synonym, antonym]
  R2.5 | Evaluation and application [the answer needs the student's OWN judgement beyond the text: suggest a measure of your own; give your view; how a finding can be applied in real life]

  G5.1 | Statements [report a statement; said that, told that, replied, informed, added that; "Report the dialogue" when the line reported is a statement]
  G5.2 | Questions [report a question; asked if, whether, wh-word; enquired, wondered; word order of a reported question]
  G5.3 | Commands and Requests [report an order, request, advice, suggestion or exclamation with a to-infinitive or "not to"; ordered, told, requested, advised, urged, suggested, begged, forbade]
G6 | Other Grammar and Usage [items set in gap-filling or editing tasks that test none of G1 to G5 (not on the CBSE grammar list)]
  G6.1 | Prepositions [in, on, at, into, between, among, upto, onto, since or for as prepositions of time is G1; preposition errors]
  G6.2 | Active and Passive Voice [the error is an active form where a passive is needed or the reverse (is trapping → is trapped, must be disinfect → must be disinfected, is made → makes); transform to the passive; options that differ in voice (was registered / is register / have registered)]
  G6.3 | Non-finites [gerund after a preposition (thank you for considering, by connecting, for avoiding → to avoid); infinitive (to + verb) and bare infinitive (let us … ensure); participle phrases (having checked); parallel verb forms after "and" in a list or slogan]
  G6.4 | Word Choice and Word Forms [choose the correct word from confusable or related words (migrate, immigrate, emigrate; mandatory, obligatory; communication, conveyance); noun, verb, adjective or adverb form of a word (cooperate → cooperation, assist → assistance, hospitable → hospitality); singular or plural form of a NOUN (parent → parents, wrinkle → wrinkles); comparison of adjectives and adverbs (degree); spelling errors]
  G6.5 | Pronouns, Relative Clauses and Connectors [pronouns (who, whom, whose, which, that, it, they, themselves); relative clauses; conjunctions and linkers (although, because, so, unless, but); clause transformation; sentence reordering]

## How to map a question

Work through these steps for every row.
1. Decide the AREA. An unseen passage that is not from a prescribed book → R1 or R2. A grammar task (gap-filling, editing, error correction, transformation, reporting) → G. A letter or analytical paragraph → W. Anything that quotes, names or is about a prescribed chapter, poem, character or author → FF, FP or FW.
2. For literature, identify the text. Use the title in brackets when it is given; otherwise identify it from characters, quotations or events. Never choose a text because of a shared theme alone.
3. Decide the topic inside that text with the rules below. Write down the event, line or idea the answer depends on, and find the topic whose key terms contain it.
4. Compare the neighbours of your choice and list in "considered" every ID you weighed.
5. Decide whether a secondary topic is genuinely needed. Most single-text questions have none.

## Mapping rules

1. Unseen passages. Questions on an unseen passage are always R1 or R2, even when the passage is about a writer, a festival, a scientist or an animal that also appears in a prescribed book. A passage is R2 when it carries a table, chart, graph, survey or other numerical or visual input, or is a factual report built on figures; otherwise R1. Each sub-question then goes to the topic of the skill it tests.
2. A row that contains only a passage or an extract and asks nothing gets the chapter of that passage or text, topic_id null, secondary_topic_ids empty, confidence "high" (even if it is cut off), syllabus_status "in_syllabus", and reason "passage only".
3. Prose extract questions (literature). Every sub-question on an extract from a prose chapter or play maps to the topic whose key terms contain the extract, whatever skill it tests (meaning, vocabulary, fill in the blank, character trait, MCQ). Exception: if the sub-question asks about something outside the extract (what happens later, the whole story's theme, the title), map it where that content lies.
4. Poem questions. Decide by what the answer has to explain:
   - WHAT the poem says → FPn.1: the meaning of a line, word, image or symbol (what the crow and hemlock represent; what "roaming barefoot" signifies; what "pads of velvet" suggests about the movement); what happens; the feelings, state of mind or tone of the speaker or of a character IN the poem (the tiger's feelings; Amanda's tone while daydreaming); the message, lesson or theme.
   - HOW the poet writes it → FPn.2: naming or identifying a figure of speech or device (simile, metaphor, personification, alliteration, repetition, symbol, transferred epithet); the rhyme scheme; structure and form; the POET's tone; the mood or atmosphere the lines create; why the poet uses a device or word and its effect.
   "Why does the poet describe X as '…'", "why does the poet use the word …", "what does the repetition of … suggest", "the effect of …" → FPn.2, because they ask about the poet's choice. "What does X stand for / represent / signify" → FPn.1.
   If a question asks both (for example "how does the poet use symbols to convey the theme", "how does the contrast enhance the theme"), the device named in the question is primary (FPn.2) and the theme is secondary (FPn.1).
5. Episode versus whole text. If the answer depends on one incident, speech, dialogue or part of a chapter, map that part's topic, even if the question uses words such as "character", "theme", "reveal", "significance" or "justify" (for example "What does Valli's refusal of the drink tell you about her?" → FF7.3; "Why did Hari Singh go back to Anil?" → FW2.2; "Explain the significance of the word 'wound' used by Mandela" → FF2.2). Map the "Whole text" topic only when the answer must draw on several parts: character development or transformation (from … to …), a character's traits shown throughout, the overall theme or message, the title, the author's overall method (humour, irony) across the text, or a comparison with another text. The key terms of a part are examples, not a complete list: an event or line that is not listed belongs to the part where it happens in the text.
6. Apply this order and stop at the first match:
   (a) the question asks about change, growth, transformation, evolution or development ("from … to", "evolves", "before and after", "trace the journey"), a relationship that develops, or a trait shown "always" or "throughout" → the "Whole text" topic;
   (b) the question names or quotes ONE event, line, speech or scene → that part (rule 5), even if it then asks what this shows about character or theme;
   (c) the question names TWO events from different parts → the first named as primary, the other as secondary;
   (d) the question names no event and asks about a character, theme, lesson, humour or message in general → the "Whole text" topic.
7. Intertextual questions (two texts). The text named FIRST in the question is primary: its chapter_id and topic_id. Choose each text's topic with rules 4 and 5 as if it were asked alone: the "Whole text" topic (or FPn.1 for a poem) when the question is about a character, theme or journey across the text; a specific part when one event is named. The second text's topic goes in secondary_topic_ids. Never list the second text's chapter as chapter_id. If the question wrongly places a text inside another chapter (for example "'Letter to God' and 'The Black Aeroplane' from 'Stories About Flying'"), map each text where it really is.
8. Multi-story chapters. Two Stories about Flying and Glimpses of India have one topic per story; they have no "Whole text" topic. Map to the story the question is about. A question about both stories of a chapter → the first story named as primary and the other as secondary.
9. Grammar. Map the rule a student must apply to get the item right, not the topic of the sentence. Look at what changes between the options or between the error and its correction:
   - the time of the action changes (enjoyed / will enjoy, is held / will be held) → G1;
   - a singular verb changes to a plural or the reverse because of the subject → G3;
   - an article or quantifier changes (a, an, the, much, more, many, few, each, all) → G4;
   - a modal changes or is needed (could, must, should, would, might) → G2;
   - direct speech is reported, or the item says "report", "complete the reported speech" → G5.1, G5.2 or G5.3 by the kind of sentence reported. A dialogue that reports a statement and a question together → the part the blank asks for; if the blank covers both, use G5 (parent);
   - an active form changes to a passive or the reverse (is trapping → is trapped) → G6.2;
   - a gerund, infinitive or bare infinitive is needed (for considering, to go, let us … ensure) → G6.3;
   - a preposition changes (between → among, upto → into) → G6.1;
   - a word is replaced by a related or confusable word, or its word class changes → G6.4;
   - a pronoun, relative word or conjunction changes → G6.5.
   If two of these change at once, map the one the answer key's correction is about. When no answer key is given, use the smallest correction that makes the sentence right: a verb that disagrees with its subject in number or person is always G3 (they demands → G3). Add a second rule as secondary only if a student must apply it separately (for example "Since morning the group of students ___ excited" needs tense and agreement: G1 with G3).
   Ignore a misleading instruction line: if the stem says "correct form of the verb" but the options are prepositions, map by the options (G6.1).
10. Writing. Letters → W1 by purpose: a letter to an editor is W1.4 whatever its subject; a letter to any other authority about a PROBLEM that the task describes (inadequate, poor, lack of, rising, broken, irregular, unsafe, noise, garbage, anxiety among students), asking for it to be fixed, is W1.1 even when the task says "suggest" or "request"; a request for information is W1.2; an order is W1.3; a letter to an authority that seeks permission, proposes, volunteers, shares plans, appreciates or invites, with no problem described, is W1.5. Analytical paragraphs → W2.1 if the input has numbers (percentages, figures, a graph, a table of values); W2.2 if the input is words (features, details, cues, a comparison of features, a process or a map).
11. MCQ: map the concept tested by the stem and the correct answer. Ignore distractor options.
12. Assertion–Reason in a reading passage → R1.2 or R2.3. In literature → the topic that decides whether the Assertion is true.
13. Alternatives (OR) and merged rows. If (a) and (b) arrive as separate rows, map each row on its own. If both alternatives, or two sub-questions, are inside one row, map the first as primary and list the other's topic as secondary when it differs.
14. Truncated or garbled text. Map from what is present and never invent the rest. Lower confidence to "medium" only if the missing words could change the topic.
15. Section letters, question numbers and marks only give the type and weight of a question, never the chapter. A 6-mark question can still be about one episode.
16. Reading tie-breakers. "Support / justify the statement or the writer's view with evidence from the text", "complete with a reason from the text", "according to the passage", "mention", "list" → R1.1 or R2.1. "What does the word or phrase mean / refer to", "identify the word that means or indicates" → R1.4 or R2.4. "What does the use of the phrase … suggest / tell us / imply about [the subject]", "state one inference", "the most likely reason", "what can you conclude" → R1.2 or R2.3. "Tone", "attitude", "mood", "the comparison or analogy in the sentence" → R1.3 (or R2.3 in a factual passage). Any question whose answer is a figure, percentage or rank → R2.2.
17. Textbook and workbook exercises. A language exercise printed after a chapter or in its Words and Expressions – II unit (vocabulary, idioms, phrasal verbs, relative clauses, reported speech, articles) that only drills grammar or vocabulary → the G topic it drills (vocabulary and idioms → G6.4). If it can only be answered from the chapter's text (find words in the story that describe …, who said this line), map the chapter part that contains it. Listening, speaking and project activities that ask about a chapter → that chapter's topic; those that do not → syllabus_status "not_found".
18. Author, poet and source questions ("Who wrote …?", "Name the poet", "From which lesson is this taken?") → the text's "Whole text" topic, FPn.1 for a poem, or the story's own topic in Two Stories about Flying and Glimpses of India.

## Content that is not in the prescribed books

- A chapter or poem that is not in the TAXONOMY (for example The Hundred Dresses, Animals or The Hack Driver from older editions, or any text from another class): syllabus_status "not_found", chapter_id and topic_id null.
- A question that names a prescribed text but asks about something that does not happen in it: map the nearest topic of that text, syllabus_status "partial", and say so in the reason. A question that only misquotes or loosely paraphrases the text ("why was it important to learn to hate", where the text says "if people can learn to hate, they can be taught to love") is NOT partial: map the part it refers to as "in_syllabus".
- An extract or quotation attributed to a prescribed text that is not in the textbook (for example a famous Mandela quotation from outside the chapter, "I have cherished the ideal of a democratic and free society … an ideal for which I am prepared to die"): map each sub-question to the topic of that text whose ideas it tests (here FF2.1, Mandela's ideals for the future), syllabus_status "partial", and say "extract not in the textbook" in the reason. Do not call a line "not in the textbook" just because it is missing from the key terms; do so only if you are confident the line does not occur in the chapter.
- Grammar items under G6 are not on the CBSE grammar list but are set in board papers: syllabus_status "partial".
- Writing tasks other than a formal letter or analytical paragraph (notice, email, article, story, diary entry, speech, debate, report, bio-sketch, descriptive paragraph): syllabus_status "not_found" (they are not in the Class X writing syllabus), chapter_id and topic_id null. A diary entry or speech written in the role of a character from a prescribed text is literature: map it to that text's topic with syllabus_status "in_syllabus".

## Confidence, status and reason

- confidence describes YOUR certainty only. "high": the deciding event, line or rule matches the key terms of one topic and no neighbour fits. "medium": two topics could reasonably be argued, or truncated text leaves doubt. "low": you are guessing.
- syllabus_status describes the books and syllabus only: "in_syllabus" when a topic covers it; "partial" when only part of it is covered or it is a G6 item; "not_found" when nothing covers it.
- reason: one sentence that names the exact event, line, character or grammar rule the answer depends on (for example "the answer rests on the postmaster collecting seventy pesos and signing the letter 'God'") and why that topic covers it. Do not name an event or rule that the answer does not use.
